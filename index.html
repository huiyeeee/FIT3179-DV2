Australia · NAPLAN, 2008 to 2026

Behind the NAPLAN average

Who sits the tests, who does well, and who falls behind.

What is NAPLAN?

NAPLAN is Australia’s yearly national test of reading, writing, spelling, grammar and numeracy.

Students in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9 sit it each March. Since 2023, each student lands in one of four levels, from “needs additional support” to “exceeding”.

Why look behind the average?

Results are often reported as one national number or a ranking of states.

A number cannot say who sat the test, or who is struggling. Those answers show where help is needed, so we look at them first.

Chapter 0 · Before the results

Before we look at the results: whose results are they?

NAPLAN is meant to test every student in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9. But the results only describe the students who actually sat the tests. So how many is that, who is missing, and is it getting worse?

The big picture

What was happening?
  • 2008 The first year of NAPLAN.
  • 2020 No tests, because of COVID-19.
  • 2022 Absences jumped: widespread illness, and natural disasters during the test window.
  • 2023 Tests moved from May to March and became fully online.
  • 2026 Tasmania: public-school teachers’ union boycott of the tests.

The data do not say why parents withdraw their children.

Who is missing?

Why this matters for the results

Students who score lower are more likely to miss the tests. AERO found that students with low Year 7 results were nearly five times as likely to miss the Year 9 tests as high performers. So the results in the chapters ahead probably look a little better than reality, most of all for these groups.

Source: AERO (2025), which also cites AERO (2023).

Key takeaways

The pattern

About 9 in 10 enrolled students have a result that counts. That share has slowly shrunk, mostly because more parents withdraw their children.

Who is missing

Students are missing most often in remote areas, or if they are Indigenous, or have parents with less schooling. Keep that in mind for the results ahead.

Chapter 1 · Overall performance

Two in three students meet expectations. Which skills they are strong in depends on the year level.

NAPLAN is not a pass or fail test. Every student is placed in one of four proficiency levels, and “Strong” is the level expected for their year. So how many get there, and does it depend on what they are tested on?

Chapter 3 · Who is being left behind?

Family and place set the gaps, and they have not moved.

Students from different backgrounds meet expectations at very different rates. Who is behind, by how much, and where does it matter most? A gap is the difference between two groups in the share of students meeting expectations, in percentage points.

Who is behind?

Place, parents and Indigenous status split results. Gender and language barely do.

Conclusion

The average hides who is missing and who is behind

Three things to take from the numbers.

  1. 1

    Not every student is counted.

  2. 2

    The average is steady, but one place stands apart.

  3. 3

    Place and family set the gaps, and they have not moved.

What the data cannot tell us
  • Causes. Remoteness, parents’ education and Indigenous status overlap, so these are patterns, not proof.
  • Missing students. Students who miss the tests tend to score lower, so the true picture is probably weaker.
  • Long trends. The four levels began in 2023, so earlier years cannot be compared.

The average says how the country is doing. The gaps say who is not.
