Chapter 3 · Equity and student background

Who is being left behind?

NAPLAN gives every student the same test, but not every student starts from the same place. Here we compare students by their parents’ education and jobs, where they live, and whether they are Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander – and ask how big the gaps are, and whether they are closing.

Throughout this chapter, “Strong or above” is our yardstick. NAPLAN places each student at one of four levels: Needs additional support, Developing, Strong or Exceeding. “Strong” means a student is meeting the expected standard for their year level.

3.1 · Background matters far more than gender

Each dot is a group of students. The further it sits from the line, the further that group is from the Australian average. Parental education, parental occupation and remoteness pull results apart by 30 to 50 points; gender barely moves them.

Why parents matterParents’ education and occupation are widely used as stand-ins for the resources around a child: books and conversation at home, help with homework, tutoring, and the quality of nearby schools.
A surpriseStudents from families that speak a language other than English at home score at or above average. Researchers link this partly to the high qualifications of many migrant parents.
GenderThe gender gap flips by subject: girls tend to lead in writing and reading, boys edge ahead in numeracy at older year levels. Try the Domain buttons.

3.2 · Behind the averages: how many students need extra help?

Splitting each group across all four levels shows who is at risk. The orange side is where students have not yet reached the expected standard.

What “needs additional support” meansThese students are not yet meeting the learning expectations for their year level and are likely to struggle with later schooling unless they get targeted help.
A staircase, not a cliffResults step down gradually from the highest to the lowest group. Disadvantage here is a gradient that touches almost every family, not only a small minority.

3.3 · Where are the gaps widest?

Choose a comparison, then look for the darkest cells. Numeracy shows the widest gap in all four comparisons.

Why numeracy?Reading and spelling are practised at home, in conversation and through books. Maths is mostly learned at school, so a student who falls behind has fewer chances to catch up outside class.
Spelling is the exceptionIt shows the smallest gaps for most comparisons – one of the few areas where students from different backgrounds finish closer together.

3.4 · Distance is not the whole story

It is tempting to blame remoteness for the Indigenous gap. Splitting each area by Indigenous status shows something different: in every area the Indigenous students sit well to the left of their non-Indigenous classmates, and the distance grows with remoteness.

What “remote” meansThe Australian Bureau of Statistics grades areas from major cities to very remote by road distance to services such as schools, hospitals and shops.
Extra hurdles in very remote areasMany Indigenous students there speak an Aboriginal language or Kriol at home, so a test in Standard Australian English is partly a second-language test. Schools also face teacher shortages and high staff turnover.
Read with careSmall dots mean few students. Very remote groups are small, so their results can swing more from year to year.

3.5 · The Indigenous gap across the country

Every state and territory has a gap, but its size varies. Each tile shows the gap, and the two results behind it (Indigenous vs non-Indigenous students at Strong or above).

Why a tile map?On a normal map the ACT would be invisible and Western Australia would dominate. Giving each jurisdiction the same-sized tile lets every one be compared fairly.
The Northern TerritoryAround 4 in 10 Year 9 students tested in the Territory are Indigenous, and many attend remote schools, so the gap here reflects the system as a whole, not one small group.
Small numbersThe ACT tests only about 200 Indigenous students per year level, so its gap can shift noticeably from year to year.

3.6 · Are the gaps closing?

Since 2023 the picture has barely moved. The Indigenous gap is narrowing slowly; the gaps for parental education, parental occupation and remoteness are essentially flat.

Why start in 2023?NAPLAN introduced a new scale, four proficiency levels and earlier testing (March) in 2023. Results before then cannot be fairly compared, so four years is all the comparable history we have.
A rough sense of speedAt the 2023–2026 pace of about half a point a year, the Indigenous gap would take roughly 70 years to close. This is an illustration, not a forecast.

What we learned

  1. Family background is the strongest dividing line. A student’s chance of reaching the expected standard can differ by 30 to 50 points depending on parents’ education, parents’ work, or where the student lives.
  2. Disadvantages stack up. Indigenous students in remote areas face the widest gaps, and the gaps are widest in numeracy and in the Northern Territory.
  3. Little has changed. Gaps have been stubbornly stable since 2023, so closing them will take deliberate, sustained effort.

Chapter 4 · Progress over time

Is the picture changing?

NAPLAN has now been run on the same scale for four years, from 2023 to 2026. So is Australian students’ performance improving, slipping or standing still? The short answer: the national average has hardly moved, but underneath it things are shifting.

As before, “Strong or above” means a student is meeting the expected standard for their year level. We also track the two ends of the scale: students who need additional support and students who are exceeding expectations.

4.1 · Some areas improved, more slipped

Each line connects a domain’s 2023 result to its 2026 result, one panel per year level. Switch to “Needs additional support” to see the struggling end instead.

Is that a real change?Each year’s test is taken by a different group of students, so small wobbles of a point or two are normal. Look for changes that are large or that keep going in the same direction.
Year 9 is differentAbout 89–90% of Year 9 students sit the tests, against roughly 94–95% in Years 3, 5 and 7, so Year 9 results describe a slightly smaller share of the cohort.

4.2 · The two ends are growing

An average can hide what is happening at the edges. Here each path follows one year level from 2023 to 2026. Moving right means more students struggling; moving up means more students excelling.

Spreading outAcross all domains the share of students at “Strong” (the middle of the scale) shrank by about 1.6 points, while both the lowest and the highest groups grew. Results are becoming more spread out.
Grammar and readingSelect these domains. Grammar shows a clear move to the right (more students needing support), and reading drifts right while the top group stays flat or shrinks – a double loss.
NumeracySelect it: the top group grows while the struggling group barely changes – the healthiest pattern on the chart.

4.3 · What moved the national average?

The average share of students at “Strong or above” fell by less than half a point. This waterfall splits that change into each domain’s contribution.

How to read itStart at zero (2023). Each bar floats from where the previous one ended: down for domains that pulled the average down, up for those that pushed it back. The last bar is the total.
Cancelling outGains in numeracy and spelling were outweighed by losses in grammar, reading and writing. A flat average can hide opposing trends.

4.4 · Who has lost ground?

Chapter 3 showed that the share of students reaching “Strong” barely changed for any group. Looking at average scores tells a sharper story: each panel compares a more advantaged group with a less advantaged one, measured against their own 2023 result.

Who is compared?Parents with a bachelor degree vs Year 11 or below; senior managers and professionals vs parents not in paid work; major cities vs very remote; non-Indigenous vs Indigenous students.
Why does this differ from Chapter 3?The “Strong” cut-off only counts whether a student crosses one line. Average scores also pick up falls among students who were already well below it, so they reveal movements the percentage hides.
Volatile small groupsVery remote students dipped by about 10 points in 2025, then recovered part-way in 2026. Small groups swing more, so judge them by the direction over several years.

4.5 · Do all states follow the same pattern?

Each tile shows how a state or territory’s share of students at “Strong or above” changed between 2023 and 2026. Teal means up; orange means down.

Changes are small next to differencesNo state moved by more than about 2 points on average, yet the Northern Territory sits roughly 30 points below the ACT and Victoria. Where a student lives still matters far more than how the year’s results moved.
Why is the NT so low?The Territory has a much larger share of remote and Indigenous students than other jurisdictions, groups that Chapter 3 showed face the widest gaps.

What we learned

  1. The headline hasn’t moved, but the shape has. The share at “Strong or above” dipped slightly, while both the struggling and the excelling groups grew.
  2. Domains are pulling in different directions. Numeracy improved, especially in Years 5 and 7, while grammar and reading lost ground.
  3. Students with less advantaged backgrounds lost the most. Average scores for students whose parents finished Year 11 or below fell by around six points, while those of degree-educated families held steady.
  4. State differences dwarf the changes. Over four years, the gap between jurisdictions has barely shifted.
